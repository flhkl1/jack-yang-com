*This was my college essay. I like it enough to put it here.*

As I survey the colourful mess of holds, my limbs subconsciously break into gesticulation. When I then envision myself on the wall, an imaginary doppelgänger leaps from my animated body and onto the holds, mimicking my every movement on the ground like a voodoo ritual. Heel hook onto sloper. Index and middle finger find razor-thin crimp. Finally, hard throw to overhang, and finish.

I’ve always likened [bouldering](https://www.instagram.com/v3_on_slab) to a flexible, spontaneous dance. Just as the backbone of a prima ballerina’s grace is painstakingly precise choreography, the foundation of a climber’s fluid execution lies in an equally painstaking process called route reading. When I route read, I first assess the whole problem, considering body positioning, style and opportunities for active rest. Then, I analyse individual moves, projecting myself onto the wall completing every move. Finally, I scrutinise each hold, observing surface material, angle and depth, sometimes to determine exact finger position. Within a minute, I will have a beta. A beta is a climber’s choreography: a mental image of exactly how I will crawl, leap and thrust my way up to the route’s apex.

Route reading complete, I glue myself to the wall and begin climbing. As I approach the crux of the route, I slowly inch my two fingertips, their surfaces now the consistency of raw chicken, onto a hold the width of three credit cards. I desperately heave my body upwards, and my left hand lodges itself into the last overhang, just as my doppelgänger’s did. Then, both legs quivering, I slip and fall, crashing into the mat with a thud.

I failed the route, but sore skin aside, I’m unscathed. Bouldering is the epitome of practising to fail. Sometimes, I flow through the route like water. Other times, my initial beta sends me careening into the mat. In bouldering, failure is always a catalyst for me to reflect on what I did wrong before I inevitably try again.

Aside from maintaining physical fitness and honing my mental acuity, bouldering also offers me a chance to reflect on stressful events. Having failed my first attempt, I crawl off the mat and gather my breath, and my mind drifts two days back in time. I was in my Sunday best at Hong Kong City Hall for the grand finals of Hong Kong’s biggest debate competition.

I had prepared for more than two weeks, envisioned myself onstage and extensively practised my speech, just as in bouldering I would read a route, project myself onto the wall and formulate a watertight beta.

As I approached the mic, though, my mind dissolved. The pressure nailed me to the spot. My legs started violently shaking, just as they so often do before the climbing wall rejects me onto the mat. I forgot to rebut an entire set of our opposition’s arguments and finished a full minute short of my time. We lost that grand final.

When I boulder, I can retry the route as many times as I’d like. In life, however, there won’t always be second chances. As I stare to my left, I watch gym staff hastily dismantling a section of the wall, replacing old routes with newer, harder, fresher ones. That’s when I realise something. Even though I’ll never get to try those same routes again, there will be newer, more difficult and fulfilling ones. Likewise, there will always be a new challenge for me to undertake and grow from.

I chalk up and step back onto the mat.
