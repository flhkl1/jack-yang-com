"I'm the well-trained fruit tree. Full of well-trained feelings and abilities and all of them grafted onto me — all bearing for someone else to pick.” - Gurney Halleck, DUNE

When I got wind yesterday that my Muay Thai coach, Kru Phat, was visiting Hong Kong, I dropped what I was doing and headed over immediately.

Muay Thai, or Thai Boxing, is a martial art and competitive sport that allows punches, kicks, elbows and knees. It’s brutal and beautiful, a dynamic sport that builds tradition and camraderie. I had the privilege of experiencing a fight camp in Chiang Mai during my sophomore year in college, where my goal eventually evolved from experiencing fight culture to participating in, and then winning, an arena fight. Kru Phat was the coach who would eventually be in my corner and guide me to that goal.

Kru Phat has dedicated his life to Muay Thai. One look at his legs reveals a shinguard of scar tissue and dense, thickened bone underneath, the remnants of more than 300 professional fights and countless more hours of deliberate practice. Fighting as a Muay Femur, he adopts a combat style defined by the use of brains and strategy, a dying breed in a fight culture and scoring system that increasingly rewards a brand of brawn and physicality that is more effective at drawing newer viewers to the sport. And he is a coach who has had multiple fighters (including his younger brother) winning titles and accolades in Muay Thai’s greatest arena, Rajadammern Stadium in Bangkok.

But none of that’s the point.

I think the history books are full of storied and accomplished athletes, fighters, and even Femurs. Great athlete-coaches are rarer still. But coaches who inspire and develop without suppressing individuation, while caring about their athletes, is a very rare breed indeed. It’s the kind of character that makes it easy for me and many of his former students to come see him at 2AM on a Monday, or to offer to help him set up a franchise in Switzerland pro bono.
