I understand *Data Science at the Singularity* by David Donoho to be a call-to-arms arguing for a tripartite push in all research areas towards building and embodying the principles of what he calls frontier research exchanges (FRXs), constituting the pillars of data sharing, code sharing, and open challenges which facilitate the rapid transmission, evaluation and adoption of the best ideas, and to tackle the issues of cynicism and dataset, availability and challenge bias, etc.

Terry Tao’s *Mathematics in the Age of AI* sounds to me like a call towards affirming the human side of AI, but I think may be better informed by the former’s suggestions. It is a hypothetical analysis conditioned on the assumption of a future in which a reasonable number of AI models will be able to tackle a reasonable number of open problems with reasonable correctness and efficiency. It draws parallels between how AI is currently upheaving the field of mathematics to the intellectual discovery of Goedel's Incompleteness problem and the Russell Paradox in the early 1900s, and the resulting crisis and formation of more robust axioms in the field.

Tao asks what changes need to be made to the mathematics community – in specific goals and values – of the enterprise of mathematical research? Tao draws upon themes like Goodhart’s Law, a 1930’s monetary policy observation that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, to illustrate the importance of considering the intrinsic incentives of mathematics as a discipline. Tao mentioned in proofs for example that human friction, so much of which is automated away by AI, is valuable signal for a more human understanding of mathematics, and that if our goal as mathematicians is to enable people to think more clearly and effectively about math then consensus is important, and canonisation, a slow process, is more important still.

The ties to machine learning are obvious here. In understanding and applying the technology of deep learning from a mechanistic, intellectually conservative view, we figuratively internalise and wield the grammar and language to be able to write prose and fiction and build intellectual movements. That is the parallel. The order of magnitude of difficulty is obvious, it is at least an OOM harder than traditional languages, a structure that requires much foundation, and with a timeframe of learning deeply compressed into one semester at least for this course. Expectations should be duly set. The motivation is clear from every side. One conclusion is that for such an intellectually rigorous domain as mathematics, we will have to rethink our humanistic systems for credit assignment and production and how we will derive meaning in all of our structures etc. This is a necessarily interdisciplinary field, the beginnings of which we have seen in Terry Tao’s epistemologically humble article. But it will take not only philosophical greats but boots-on-the-ground builders and engineers to execute upon this vision and to bring knowledge development for humanity into its next phase. I see myself as being more emboldened to understand this world and its language, and to play vanguard to this development, whether that be in the form of a company that I start or to embody this ideology in some other manner.
