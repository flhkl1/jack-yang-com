AI in the world of bits has gotten very smart. By 2026, scaling laws made frontier LLMs extremely good at a few prominent domains (coding, math) out-of-the-box. Some startups niched in on more involved tasks (personal assistance, back-office business functions). A good fraction of these startups I generally expect to fail if they don’t succeed at building trust and customer relationships faster than models improve and frontier lab FDE teams can execute. There is good reason to believe that this will happen, mostly because these frontier labs are so cash-rich and generally attract the best talent in the world.

Physical AI will follow a similar exponential. In August, Generalist released GEN-1.5, a one-shot learner. Aside from architectural disagreements, roboticists are generally optimistic that scaling laws will continue to hold. We can reason today about the improvements to be experienced by models that interact with the real world (physical AI), and how this will affect the relative value of business functions moving forward.

The confluence of these two trends will change the relative value of business functions towards ownership of customer relationships and deployment experience. First of all, it’s clear that as of today, scaling distribution for these models and ensuring alignment is the dominant problem. But once this problem is solved, in parallel with improvements to be made in general intelligence, it’s reasonable to suggest the marginal cost of physical intelligence will drastically decrease. It’s also reasonable to suggest that in such a world, AI in the world of bits will become sufficiently capable that the relative value of business functions that are predominantly created through reasoning and not reality-tethered operations like in-person execution, trust and connections will also decrease.

We started by thinking of a marketplace for hardware to capitalize on these trends. We started Unbox with the thesis that hardware was the ultimate bottleneck to deployment. We wanted to be the Amazon for robotics and provide a comprehensive cross-border hardware marketplace that basically arbitraged price differentials. After speaking to multiple stakeholders in the robotics industry, including countless engineers working on deployment across companies within robotics healthcare it was clear that the bottleneck to scaling that they wanted to talk about were software validation and deployment issues, i.e. we have robot parts, they work reliably up to the firmware level, how do we set them up to do the actual task and troubleshoot continuously as we scale?

The obvious play here, listening to our customers, would be to actually build the software layer that solved these software validation and deployment issues. There are a number of reasons why I think this is not the right approach given our current circumstances.

First of all, we are not going to build smart models to handle generalized physical intelligence. This domain belongs to the incredibly talented and smart people at companies like Pi and Generalist. We are also not validation and deployment software engineers. This seems contrary to what our market research suggests but we see a few problems with going this route. The first problem is currently also being tackled by incredibly smart people like Matthew who have been working on this problem for a very long time. Lots of companies already have internal systems that more or less solve this problem (e.g. Tesla). In this regime, we are already very late. More importantly, with our current team comp (FAANG SWE, global supply chain and ops, consulting clubs in Econ + DS at Berkeley), we cannot expect to even begin competing in this domain against physicists, firmware engineers and ML researchers. It is not feasible whatsoever.

If our thesis that in-person execution, trust and connections, as well as ownership of the underlying business relationships and being as close to the source of value creation as possible is important, our company must necessarily move towards deployment and finding ways to capture long-term value from these customer relationships. Specifically, we must operate in high-value deployment and execution of robots.

Robotics deployment is a sufficiently broad space that deployment plays can currently be very orthogonal. For example, a robot company that specializes in deploying, end-to-end, and maintaining robots for elder care is sufficiently differentiated at this current stage to companies that focus on doing the same but for manufacturing facilities. This will come around after I make the below point.

Auditing the space, which is relatively new, there are a lot of companies that are working at different levels of the deployment problem for robotics. For example, European-based microAGI is working on full-stack hardware provision all the way down to training models and deploying engineers on-site for testing and validation, bring-up etc. A few barriers currently stand in the way towards deployment, at a relatively high-level, stated by a member of that team. Some are real, and most are not. The first stated risk / pain point is that embodied foundation models in their current states are just not good enough to be sufficiently fast, although the gradient on their capabilities is something approximating an early-stage exponential. Right now, this issue bottlenecks deployment speed and means you need to hire really smart engineers but this is probably going to change eventually if models get smarter. We see this trend as continuing and needing no intervention on our end.

The second problem is political; it’s a hard sell for CEOs to adopt a nascent and unproven technology and lay off a workforce if there are no guarantees on performance. A lot of industries that such a model would work in have physical tasks that make robot deployments valuable (e.g. unions that advocate for safety, long hours, worker protection) and this is precisely correlated with the presence of unions that make adoption of these technologies more difficult. This yields a second prior about our solution, which is that it would have to either be so good in terms of plug-and-play the robot into your system unit economics that not adopting it is an actively losing play, or offer unions some compensation and financial or political incentive to be onboard with them thinking that they will basically lose their jobs, or some creative middle ground in between where you reframe the issue not as one of job loss but then you have to give them something tangible to get them on board with this framing. This is more a statement about the unit economics of our deployment model, which with creative financing could be solved (for example as a RaaS model that allows CEOs to justify experimental spend as OpEx, creative financing, custom models where we tradeoff data gathering and collection in sensitive industries for financial discounts, etc.)

The third problem relates to geopolitical and supply chain inefficiencies. There are a lot of parts right now that companies would like to deploy, that carry geopolitical baggage with implementation that is completely out of any one corporation’s control (e.g. deploying Chinese humanoids is out of the question). This points to the original idea of hardware plays, which is a deeply fragmented space and requires multinational presence for obvious reasons. The obvious solution to this would be some level of hidden vertical integration play, which past experience points to as being useful generally when doing any physical enterprise (see Elon Musk’s companies)

A core realization about the companies that currently operate in this space is that they are focused on deployment and optimization of that process as the fundamental goal, i.e. we want to deploy the most robots like a billion odd robots by 2035 was the stated intention of one of the heads at microAGI. The obligation of a robot deployment company is to its customer. We cannot start by serving everybody, so we have to niche in on the select few fields where we could deploy robots for them where the unit economics make sense.
